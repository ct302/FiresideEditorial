## The Log That Waits

There is a particular hush that falls when the last guest departs and the house settles into its winter skin. You've washed the dishes, folded the napkins, and drawn the curtains against the dark. Now you kneel before the hearth—not to warm your hands, exactly, but to remember something older than memory. The Yule log was never merely fuel. It was a promise laid across iron grates, a chunk of oak or ash selected weeks before, sometimes wrapped in ivy, sometimes kissed with ale or sprinkled with salt. You lit it from the embers of last year's fire, if you were keeping faith with the old ways, and let it burn slow through the longest night.

You would hear it before you saw it—the soft crackle of sap bursting, the dry pop of bark surrendering. The scent is not pine or cinnamon but something deeper: wood sugar and ancient carbon, the smell of time turning to ash. Smoke curls in slow ribbons, clinging to wool sweaters and the backs of books. Heat radiates unevenly, drawing you close, then pushing you back, a rhythm as steady as breathing.

> A fire is not tamed. It is negotiated with, hour by hour, log by log, until dawn steals the last orange from the coals.

## Keeping the Embers

I am of the opinion—gently held, I hope—that we have traded too much of this stillness for convenience. A gas flame offers instant heat without the labor of stacking, without the ash to sweep at morning. But it does not offer the same companionship. A real hearth demands your attention. You must tend it. You must listen when it hisses, adjust when it sighs, feed it when it dims. In that tending, you find a kind of prayer too quiet for words.

Children used to sit with their backs to the blaze, faces lit in flickering amber, while elders told stories that stretched back generations. Now you might sit alone with a book, or with someone you love in comfortable silence. The company matters less than the presence. The fire watches over you while you watch over it.

When the log finally collapses into white-grey ash, you do not rush to clean it. You let the embers glow, small and stubborn, until they too surrender to the dark. There is a peace in that surrender, a reminder that even the warmest things must end.

So tonight, if you have a hearth—or even a single candle placed near a window—light it slowly. Let the match sputter. Watch the wood catch. Breathe in the smoke. Let the evening be long, and the fire longer.

The longest night is not something to endure. It is something to sit beside, warm and watchful, until the light returns of its own accord.
