## The Wood Remembers

You have felt it — that particular hush when the last ember of November surrenders to December's dark. The house contracts. Floorboards sigh. And somewhere, if you're lucky, a match strikes.

The Yule log was never merely fuel. In the old way, it was chosen months ahead: oak for endurance, ash for prosperity, birch for new beginnings. The wood *remembered* the forest. You could smell rain on its bark, the ghost of wind through canopy. When it finally met flame, it released a year of stored sunlight.

> The log does not burn. It *unwinds*.

## The Vigil

Your grandmother knew the rules. You light it with a scrap of last year's log — continuity stitched in smoke. It must catch on the first try; a second match invites misfortune. Then you sit. You watch. The flames write their own language: orange script against black, popping sentences of sap.

The hearth becomes the room's compass. Chairs turn toward it. Voices drop. Even the cat abandons the windowsill for the rug's worn hollow. This is what the solstice asks: not celebration, exactly. *Witness*.

## Ash and Promise

By morning, the log surrenders to white ash — lighter than snow, warmer than wool. You'll save a shard for next year, wrapped in cloth scented with bay and dried orange peel. The rest goes to the garden, feeding roots that will hold next winter's wood.

The fire goes out. The darkness remains. But your fingers still tingle with heat you carried from the forest, through the solstice, into the new year's first pale light.
