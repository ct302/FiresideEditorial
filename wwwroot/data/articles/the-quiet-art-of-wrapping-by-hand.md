## A Return to the Wrapping Table

There is a particular hush that falls over a room when someone begins wrapping presents by hand. The scissors find their rhythm. The paper whispers as it folds. Somewhere beneath the ribbon spool, a small thought keeps surfacing — *this matters*. Not the gift itself, perhaps, but the hours someone spent making it feel like an arrival.

We have drifted so far from the wrapping table. Pre-taped bows. Plastic sleeves. The convenience of a bag that simply zips shut. Yet ask anyone which presents they remember most from childhood, and they will not describe the contents. They will describe the sound the paper made. The way the twine bit into the kraft. The sprig of rosemary tucked beneath the bow.

Handmade gift wrapping is not about perfection. It is about presence.

## The Materials That Tell a Story

Begin with what you have. Brown kraft paper — the slightly crumpled kind, the kind that looks like it has traveled — makes an ideal canvas. It forgives uneven edges. It welcomes a handwritten name in metallic ink. It feels honest.

> A gift wrapped by hand is never just an object. It is the first sentence of a conversation.

For trimmings, look beyond the ribbon aisle. Butcher's twine, naturally dyed cotton, thin strips of linen — these humble materials do more work than any satin bow. A single dried orange slice, a sprig of pine, a star anise pod taped at the corner. Nature is generous with her packaging supplies, and she asks very little in return.

If you have children, let them stamp the paper with potato prints dipped in forest-green ink. If you have a moment alone, try your hand at the Japanese furoshiki tradition — a square of fabric folded around the gift, knotted at the top. No tape required. The fabric becomes part of the present itself.

## The Slowing Down

Perhaps the deepest gift in handmade gift wrapping is not what you give the recipient but what you give yourself. An hour with scissors, paper, and twine is a kind of meditation. The hands move. The mind quiets. The list of errands fades into the back room of the mind, where it belongs.

Wrap by candlelight, if you can manage it. Put on the record your grandmother used to play in December. Let the tape dispenser stay where it is.

There will come a moment, perhaps halfway through the second gift, when you realize you are no longer wrapping at all — you are tending to something. To the person who will receive it. To the season itself. To the small, stubborn belief that beauty lives in the handmade.

And that, more than anything inside the box, is worth giving away.
