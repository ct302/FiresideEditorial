Few winter drinks travel as well as mulled wine. It leaves the stovetop in a copper-colored pot, carrying cinnamon, clove, citrus peel, and the dark fruit of red wine. By the time it reaches your hands, the kitchen has become a small shelter against snow, rain, or the particular blue cold that gathers after supper.

## The Rhine and the Alps

In Germany, Glühwein is the bright coin of the Christmas market: a mug warming your palms while brass music and roasting almonds drift through the crowd. The home version is wonderfully plain. Warm one 750-ml bottle of dry red wine with four orange slices, eight cloves, two cinnamon sticks, two stars of anise, and a tablespoon of sugar or honey. Keep it below a simmer for 15 to 20 minutes, then strain. A spoonful of rum is a private winter luxury, not a requirement.

Austria often leans toward the same generous spice, while northern Italy’s vin brulé—especially in the Alpine regions—may glow with lemon as well as orange. The difference is rarely a law. It is a grandmother’s handwriting, a market stall’s secret, the thickness of the peel left curling in the pot.

## Nordic Glögg, Deep and Almond-Scented

Swedish glögg is more shadowed and resinous. Cardamom and ginger join the familiar cloves and cinnamon; raisins and blanched almonds wait at the bottom of the glass. For a small bowl, crush eight cardamom pods with six cloves, two cinnamon sticks, three slices of fresh ginger, and a strip of orange peel. Steep them in 250 ml of wine for 20 minutes off the heat, then strain into 500 ml of gently warmed wine. Add the almonds and raisins just before serving.

Many Swedish tables offer a splash of arrack or vodka, though the drink is already warm-hearted without it. In Finland, glögi often announces itself through cardamom’s green, perfumed snap, and it may be served as a fragrant non-alcoholic cup beside cinnamon buns. The spices are doing the remembering.

## From Paris to Prague

France’s vin chaud is usually lighter and fruit-forward: red wine, orange, a little honey, and spice kept on a low flame until the house smells like a winter orchard. Farther east, Prague’s svařené víno welcomes lemon and orange with cloves, while Poland’s grzane wino may be sweetened with honey and fortified to suit the weather. Hungary’s forralt bor carries the same family resemblance, each household deciding how boldly the citrus should speak.

> The best mulled wine is not cooked; it is persuaded—slowly, patiently, until the spices unfold without ever turning sharp.

What travels between these recipes is not merely wine. It is the pause before the first sip, the spoon tapping the rim, the decision to make one more pot when the evening grows quiet. Europe has many names for this cup, but the feeling is remarkably old and familiar: warmth held in both hands, with winter waiting outside the glass.
