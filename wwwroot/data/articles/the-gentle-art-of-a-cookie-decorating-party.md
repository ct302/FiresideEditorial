## A Table That Welcomes You

The secret to a memorable cookie decorating party is not flawless icing. It is the way the room smells of butter, cinnamon, and warm sugar long before anyone lifts a piping bag.

Begin with cookies that are cool, sturdy, and generously shaped. Round snickerdoodles, gingerbread stars, sugar cookies cut into little trees—each one becomes a small canvas. Keep them slightly plain, with a whisper of spice or a dusting of cinnamon sugar. Their simplicity gives the guests something to enjoy even before the decorations begin.

Set out napkins, plates, and a few sturdy trays. If you have room, place a folded cloth beneath the decorating supplies; it softens the sound of spoons and makes the table feel less like a workstation and more like a winter fireside.

> The best cookie decorating is never quite as tidy as the recipe promised.

## Arrange the Sweetest Supplies

Give each guest a small plate and a few decorating tools. Royal icing in white, chocolate, burgundy, and pale gold is enough. Add piping bags, toothpicks, tiny brushes, and bowls of sprinkles, crushed peppermint, toasted coconut, and edible pearls.

You need not provide every color in the rainbow. A few thoughtful choices encourage improvisation. A cookie with a crooked snowflake can become a snowdrift. A smudge of icing can be covered with a berry, a star, or a playful little face.

Place the supplies in clusters rather than one long row. This leaves room for conversation, for passing the sprinkles, and for the occasional accident. A spilled scoop of sugar is rarely a disaster; it is often the moment everyone laughs about later.

For drinks, offer cocoa, spiced cider, tea, or a small glass of something crisp. Keep the beverages easy to hold, because hands will soon be busy. A bowl of extra napkins belongs near the center of the table, as does a small dish for used toothpicks and forgotten decorations.

## Let the Evening Soften

Choose music low enough to leave space for stories. Old carols, quiet jazz, or a familiar winter playlist will do. The songs should feel like background firelight, not an announcement.

As the cookies dry, invite guests to choose their favorite. Some will admire the neatest; others will defend the most peculiar. Let them explain their creations. A lopsided reindeer may become the family legend, a blue snowflake may earn a place on the kitchen window, and a cookie with too much icing may be declared a masterpiece.

Pack extras in a tin lined with parchment. Give each guest a few to take home, along with the faint scent of vanilla and the pleasure of having made something together.

Perhaps the sweetest part of the evening is not the finished cookie at all, but the memory of sitting close, laughing over small mishaps, and feeling that winter has made a place for everyone at the table.
