## The Weight of Winter Wood

There is something primal in the way a single log transforms a room. The first time you lay hands on a Yule log — truly large, still carrying the scent of pine and earth from the woodpile — you understand why our ancestors built their homes around fire. The bark crumbles slightly under your palms, rough and fragrant, and for a moment the cold seeps out of your bones just watching it catch flame.

## A Flame Passed Down

The old traditions say the Yule log must never be entirely consumed. A piece kept through the year protects the home from ill fortune. In some villages, the smallest sliver was tucked beneath the Christmas tree, or placed near the door to welcome the new year. Your grandmother might have murmured prayers over hers, or sung low songs that only the flames could carry.

> "The Yule log is more than wood burning — it is memory made visible, each flame a story your family has told a thousand times."

Today, we light ours on the shortest night, when the world outside grows sharp with frost. The log settles with a hush, sending up sparks that rise like small wishes. Around us, the room softens — the harsh edges of December give way to something gentler. You pour cocoa, someone passes the cinnamon sticks, and for an hour at least, the hearth holds everyone who matters.

## Keeping the Fire Alive

Not all of us have stone hearths now. Some gather round electric flames that flicker convincingly, or candles arranged in the old Yule log shape. The tradition persists, because what is Christmas without the promise of warmth? In kitchens and apartments, the ritual adapts — a log-shaped cake, a single candle burning through the twelve days, the act of gathering itself becoming the flame.

The log will burn down eventually. But the stories it holds — they last well beyond the ashes.
