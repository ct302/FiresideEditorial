## A Quiet Table Glow

Christmas, in many homes, begins not with the first carol but with the first small surrender of wax. By late afternoon, the kitchen smells of oranges, resinous pine, and something faintly sweet as the stove is lit. On the table, bare branches wait for a little more light. A row of homemade candles can give the evening a warmth that no ceiling fixture quite manages.

Set them among the greenery, and the room changes. Candlelight catches the glossy leaves, the rim of a crystal bowl, the rough weave of a linen runner. It turns every plate into a small theatre and every face into something gentler.

The pleasure is not only in the finished glow. It lies in the waiting, the pouring, the quiet concentration of watching wax become something new.

> A handmade candle carries the memory of its making whenever it is lit.

## Wax, Wicks, and Winter

Begin simply. Soy wax is forgiving, fragrant, and easy to work with; a soy-paraffin blend gives a firmer snap and a clearer release of scent. Choose cotton or wood wicks suited to the diameter of your container, for a candle is only as happy as its wick.

Melt the wax gently in a double boiler. Resist the urge to hurry. High heat can darken the wax, dull its scent, and make it behave like a stubborn child. When it has become a clear, amber liquid, let it cool until the edges turn slightly cloudy.

While it rests, secure the wick in the centre of a clean jar or small tin. A dab of wax, a pair of tweezers, or a wick holder will keep it upright. Add fragrance only when the wax has cooled enough to preserve it. A few drops of cedar, cinnamon, clove, or orange peel will scent the room without overwhelming the meal.

Pour slowly, leaving a little headspace at the top. Let the candle stand undisturbed for several hours, then trim the wick to about a quarter of an inch. The first burn should be allowed to soften the surface fully, so that the flame does not tunnel into the wax later.

Safety deserves its place beside the romance. Work over a heat-proof surface, keep water away from molten wax, and never leave a burning candle unattended. The holiday table is for gathering, not for anxieties.

## A Centerpiece That Breathes

Once the candles have set, dress them with winter. Tuck pine, cedar, holly, or eucalyptus around the bases, keeping greenery well away from the flames. Scatter dried citrus, cinnamon sticks, or a few glossy berries between the jars. The result should feel abundant but not crowded, as though the table has been discovered rather than decorated.

Place the candles at intervals rather than in a single line. Their light will wander across the room, touching the glassware, the folded napkins, the faces around it. If you prefer a more formal arrangement, use matching vessels and let the greenery do the talking.

When supper is finished, extinguish the candles one by one. The faint smell of wax and evergreen lingers in the fabric, the wood, the air. Outside, the dark has deepened; inside, the table still holds a little of the day's warmth.

Perhaps that is the true gift of candle-making: not a perfect centerpiece, but a reminder that light, like hospitality, is made by patient hands and shared slowly.
