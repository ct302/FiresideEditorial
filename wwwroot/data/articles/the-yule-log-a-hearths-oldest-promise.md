You know the weight of it before you ever lift it — that chosen log, dragged from the woodpile with a grunt and a grin, its bark still holding the scent of November rain. The Yule log is not merely fuel. It is a covenant.

## The Log That Remembers

In the old way, the log was selected months ahead, sometimes a whole trunk of oak or ash, set aside to season while the year turned. You would carve a notch for the previous year's charcoal, a tangible link — last winter's fire birthing this one. The log remembers every hand that placed it, every wish whispered into its flames.

> The hearth is the only altar where the offering warms the giver.

You feel it in the wrists when you lower it onto the andirons: the centuries pressing down. The first match catches not just wood but memory. Smoke curls, carrying the ghost of last year's pine needles, the sugar-glazed rind of an orange studded with cloves, the splash of cider hissing on hot stone.

## Tending the Ember

There is a rhythm to it. You do not poke; you *coax*. A bellows breathes like a sleeping beast. The flames climb slow, deliberate, licking the charred edges where runes were once scored — initials, dates, a heart. The heat spreads uneven at first, then steady, pushing back the dark that presses against the windowpanes.

You sit close enough to feel the pop of sap pockets, the sudden flare when a knot gives way. The cat claims the warmest brick. Someone's wool socks steam on the fender. This is the geometry of comfort: fire, shadow, the clink of a mug set down on a side table.

## Ashes and Promises

When the log finally collapses into itself, a mound of white ash remains — finer than flour, warmer than soil. You will save a jar of it for the garden come spring, for the next log's notch, for the slip of paper you'll burn at midnight bearing a single word: *release*.

The fire does not die. It migrates. Into the embers banked beneath the ash. Into the soot-blackened poker leaning against the mantel. Into the story you'll tell next December, when the woodpile yields another giant, and you lift it, feeling the weight of every winter that came before.
