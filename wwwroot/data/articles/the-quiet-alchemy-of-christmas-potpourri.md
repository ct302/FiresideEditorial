There is a particular hush that settles over the house in late November — the kind that arrives before the guests, before the lists, before the rush. You feel it in your fingertips when you pull the first box of ornaments from the attic. It whispers: *slow down.*

## The Scent Memory

Store-bought potpourri smells like a department store in October: sharp, synthetic, vaguely cinnamon-adjacent. But the real thing? The real thing smells like your grandmother's kitchen. Like the year you strung popcorn garlands with numb fingers. Like the night you forgot the cider on the stove and the house held that sweet, steamy ghost for days.

> Scent is the only sense wired directly to memory. No translation required. No filter. Just — *there you are*, eight years old, standing on a chair to stir the pot.

## The Gathering

You don't need a recipe. You need a walk.

Pine cones from the park path, their scales still tacky with sap. Rose hips from the neighbor's hedge, bright as coral beads. A few sprigs of rosemary from the windowsill pot, released only when you brush past. Orange peels saved from breakfast, curled like dried smiles on a rack above the radiator.

Cinnamon sticks. Whole cloves. Star anise — those perfect wooden flowers. A vanilla bean, split and scraped, the caviar reserved for custard. The hulls go in the pot.

## The Simmer

Low heat. A heavy-bottomed pot. Water to cover. You'll watch the steam rise in lazy curls, carrying the forest and the orchard and the spice route all at once. Two hours. Maybe three. The kitchen softens. Your shoulders drop.

Strain it. Spread the solids on parchment to dry — a second life as bowl potpourri, tucked into sachets, slipped into coat pockets.

The liquid? Ladle it into jars while warm. A ribbon. A label in pencil: *December. Kitchen. Us.*

## The Keeping

Weeks later, you'll open a jar in February. The steam will rise — different now, quieter — and for a moment the tree is still standing, the cider is still steaming, and everyone you love is still at the table.
