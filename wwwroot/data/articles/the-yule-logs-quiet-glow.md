## The Log That Remembers

You step into the kitchen just as the first frost curls around the windowpanes. The air smells of pine resin and dried orange peel, and the hearth waits — an empty stone cradle for a log that has been chosen with care. In many households the Yule log is not merely firewood; it is a keepsake, a piece of last year's oak or ash saved from the previous solstice, its bark still bearing the faint scar of a candle's kiss.

> "The log remembers every hand that lifted it, every wish whispered into its smoke."

When you set it in the grate, the weight feels like a promise. The flames catch the dry heartwood, and the room leans into a hush that only a true hearth can command.

## Tending the Flame

You feed the fire slowly — twigs, then split kindling, then the log itself — watching the amber tongues climb the grain. The sound is a low, steady crackle, a metronome for the night. Sprinkle a handful of rosemary or a pinch of cinnamon onto the embers; the scent rises, twining with the woodsmoke, and you feel the centuries of solstice gatherings press close. This is the moment to let the mind wander: to the stories your grandmother told, to the promises you made to yourself when the year turned.

## After the Embers Fade

When the last glow surrenders to gray ash, you scoop the remnants into a tin, labeling it with the date. Next winter you will place that tin beside the new log, a quiet continuity that stitches one solstice to the next. The house settles, the fire's memory lingering in the walls, and you understand that the true warmth was never just the flame — it was the intention you carried into it.
