At the bottom of December, the house settles into a hush. You notice the way windows go dark early, how radiators tick like sleepy insects, how the last daylight leaks under the door. Then you haul the Yule log into the kitchen, or you bank the old hearth fire, or you simply watch the one your grandmother kept alive. The tradition is not one thing. It is a family of small, stubborn acts. The cold leans against the door, and you, warm from the stove, feel like a secret.

## The Fire Begins

The log should be seasoned, perhaps oak or pine, dragged in with a rope and a story. You split it once, ceremonially, or leave it round and let the flames find the heart. The first lighting is the oldest magic: not fireworks, not presents, but a spark caught, then caught again. The room fills with the smell of resin and smoke. You wrap the log in paper and old letters, sometimes, or ribbons, sometimes. The chimney coughs. The flame catches. The Yule log burns through the longest night, and you tell stories so the dark does not feel empty. You do not rush the dark away; you learn to sit inside it.

> We do not keep the fire because we are afraid of winter. We keep it because we remember how to come home.

You feed it kindling, then logs, then leave it to burn low. The embers glow like coals from a buried sun. Someone sings off-key. Someone hangs a stocking. The dog sleeps by the hearth. The cat watches the sparks. You feel the heat on your face and the cold at the edges of the room, and somehow that is comfort, not the absence of cold, but the presence of tending.

## Keeping the Flame

There is a rhythm here: the match strike, the paper wrap, the first whoosh, the quiet roar. The Yule log is not just a log. It is a promise that the hearth will hold you. That the flame will return. That even after the power goes out, you have a way to make light. That even after the year has been heavy, you have a circle of warmth, a story, a hand on your shoulder.

There is no finish line here, only the next small flame, the next breath, the next story borrowed from someone else.

Maybe your tradition is a tiny electric log with a rotating flame. Maybe it is a live fire in a field. Maybe it is just the blue glow of a wood stove and the smell of cedar. The old ways are not rigid. They are a language you speak badly and beautifully.

You listen for the logs settling, for the wood sighing. You imagine ancestors bending to the same heat. You promise to do this again next year, though you know the doing is the point. The fire does not care about your promises, but you do, and that is enough.

As the fire cracks and settles, you realize the Yule log was never about the log. It was about the waiting, the spark, the shared breath. The long night becomes less long when someone else is there, and the hearth becomes a small sun you carry inside.

And in the morning, when the ashes cool and the room smells of smoke and pine, you will understand: you were never keeping the fire alive. You were keeping yourself.
