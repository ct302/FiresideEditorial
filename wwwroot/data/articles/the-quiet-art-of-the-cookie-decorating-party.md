## Set the Table Before You Set the Mood

There is a particular kind of warmth that gathers in a kitchen on a December afternoon — the kind that has nothing to do with the oven and everything to do with the people standing around it.

A cookie decorating party is not a baking party. That distinction matters. You do the baking ahead, alone or with one trusted companion, in the quiet hours of a morning when the house still smells like sleep. Cut your shapes — stars, trees, mittens, imperfect circles that you'll call “snowflakes” — and let them cool completely. By the time your guests arrive, the hard work is already done.

What remains is the gentle part. The icing. The sprinkles. The laughter of adults who have suddenly remembered they are allowed to make a mess.

## The Icing Should Be Stiff, the Schedule Should Not

Royal icing is your friend here, and its forgiving nature is its finest quality. Mix it thick for outlining, thin it with a few drops of water for flooding, and set out small bowls in colors that feel like winter rather than a candy store: muted sage, soft ivory, a dusty rose that looks almost frozen.

> The best cookie parties end when they end. You have not invited people over to be managed.

Set everything out on a table that doesn't mind a little sugar in its grain. Piping bags, toothpicks for fine detail, small spoons for the children or the impatient. A plate of already-decorated examples — not to impress, but to give shy hands a place to start.

Pour something warm. Mulled cider, perhaps, or a pot of tea that you refill without asking. The rhythm of the afternoon should feel like breathing.

## Let the Ugly Cookies Be Loved Most

Some cookies will be beautiful. These will be photographed and quietly set aside. Others will be wild, over-sprinkled, comically lopsided — and these are the ones people will reach for first, because they carry the proof of a good time.

Do not correct anyone. Do not rearrange the table. Do not apologize for the cookies that broke or the icing that didn't set quite right. The imperfection is the whole point.

When the afternoon softens into evening and your guests leave with paper plates of their own making, you will find the kitchen still dusted in sugar. Leave it for a little while. Sit down. Let the warmth settle into your bones before you wash a single bowl.
