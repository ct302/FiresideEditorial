The match strikes. Sulfur, then cedar. Then the first true catch — bark curling, sap hissing, the log exhaling centuries.

You know this moment. The house holds its breath. The radiator's clang falls silent. Even the cat stops kneading her blanket and turns, ears swiveling, toward the only heat that matters.

## The Wood Remembers

A proper Yule log was never mere fuel. In the old way — your grandmother's way, her grandmother's — you chose it in autumn. Oak for endurance. Ash for light. Birch for new beginnings, if you were brave enough to burn the future. You dragged it home through frost-rutted lanes, bark scarred by your mittened grip, and set it by the hearth to wait. To dry. To *witness*.

> The log remembers every hand that carried it. Every prayer whispered into its grain. Every child who pressed a cold palm to its rough skin and made a wish.

You fed it last year's ash — a pinch, reverent — so the fire never truly died. Continuity, not ceremony. The distinction matters.

## Ash and Oak, Holly and Thorn

Midnight. The log collapses inward, a cathedral of coals. This is when you write burdens on birch bark — the argument with your brother, the diagnosis, the rent notice — and slip them into the heartwood. Watch them curl black. Watch them vanish.

Holly berries tossed in for protection. A sprig of thorn for boundaries. Your mother's dried orange slices, their scent climbing the chimney like a promise: *spring exists*. The smoke writes its own language on the ceiling beams, dark calligraphy only the house can read.

## The Morning After

Dawn finds you on the hearth rug, warming your spine against the stone. The log is gone — reduced to a shallow bowl of pearl-gray ash, still warm enough to bake an apple if you buried one. You'll scatter this ash on the garden come March. On the thresholds. In the shoes of anyone leaving for war, for college, for love.

The fire doesn't solve winter. It *accompanies* you through it. Different thing entirely.

Somewhere in the walls, the house settles. A timber creaks. The cat sighhes, finally asleep. You carry the last ember to the kindling box — next year's beginning, already waiting in the dark.
