## The Longest Night Begins with Smoke

You know the feeling—that hush that falls when the sun retreats early and the world outside turns to shadow and frost. There is something deeply human about wanting to sit close to flame, to watch the same fire that warmed your grandmother’s hands and her grandmother’s before her.

A Yule log was never just wood. It was chosen with care—often oak or ash, sometimes dragged in from the forest with ceremony, anointed with cider or salt, and set to burn on the solstice so that its light might coax the sun back toward us. I like to imagine those old households, the heavy oak beam dragged across snow, the smell of bark and resin rising as it met the hearth.

## What the Fire Remembers

There is a particular sound to a hearth fire: not the roar of a bonfire, but a low, conversational crackle. Popping sap. The soft collapse of a log settling into embers. The scent is resinous and warm, clinging to wool sweaters and the pages of unread books. If you press your palm near the stone, you feel that slow, radiant heat—the kind that sinks into bones rather than just skin.

I am gently opinionated here: a gas insert cannot replicate this. It lacks the unpredictability, the need for tending, the quiet conversation between human and flame. A hearth fire asks something of you. You must stack, you must light, you must watch. You must adjust the screen when a spark leaps too close to the rug. In return, it gives you something modern life rarely offers: stillness with purpose, the kind that lets your thoughts slow to the pace of burning wood.

> To tend a fire is to keep a promise to the darkness—that you will not let it win.

When I was a child, my family kept what we called the Christmas log burning from solstice through New Year’s. We never let it die completely. A few embers, covered in ash overnight, would be coaxed back to life with breath and kindling. That continuity felt sacred, as though we were keeping a vigil not just for the season, but for each other.

## The Embers That Remain

Even now, without a great oak beam dragged from the woods, I light a small fire on the shortest day. I let the room dim, leaving only the tree lights and the hearth. I listen to the wood speak in its ancient language of pops and sighs. And in that amber glow, I remember that light does not have to be brilliant to be necessary. Sometimes it only needs to be steady, patient, unmoved by the wind outside.

The fire eventually reduces to gray ash and glowing coals. But the warmth remains in the walls, in the wool blankets, in the quiet space between heartbeats. That, perhaps, is the real tradition—not the log itself, but the gathering close, the willingness to sit in the dark and wait for the light to return.
